Causal effects of interest:
\begin{align*}
\text{Average treatment effect (ATE)}  =& \EE [Y_i (1) - Y_i (0)] \\
\text{Average treatment effect on the treated (ATT) } =& \EE [Y_i (1) - Y_i (0) \vert D_i = 1]
\end{align*}
\textbf{Problem:} You cannot observe potential outcomes $\{Y_i (0), Y_i(1)\}$ for everyone 
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Solution}: Randomization allows us to uncover certain causal effects
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Potential outcomes under randomization}


Randomization implies that the potential outcomes $\{Y_i (0),  Y_i (1)\}$ are \textbf{independent} of treatment assignment $D_i$:
\begin{itemize}
\item Two variables are independent if the occurrence of one does not impact the occurrence of the other, and vice versa
\item Nothing about your underlying potential outcomes determines likeliness to take the treatment 
\end{itemize}

\vspace{2ex}
In this example, in a lab setting, independence of treatment assignment and potential outcomes means your assignment to treatment doesn't impact your control potential outcome or treatment potential outcome, and vice versa
\begin{itemize}
\item You have randomization of the treatment \textbf{assignment}; 
\end{itemize}

\vspace{2ex}
You \emph{would not} have independence of treatment and potential outcomes if participants got to choose whether they take the drug or not
\begin{itemize}
\item Only those who wanted to grow a beard would take the drug
\item When participants can \textbf{select} whether they want to be treated,  that leads to \textbf{selection bias} when estimating causal effects
\end{itemize}

\vspace{2ex}




\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Observed differences in outcomes under potential outcome framework}

Why do we need independence of treatment assignment ($D_i$) and potential outcomes $\left( \{Y_i (0), Y_i (1)\}\right)$?
\begin{itemize}
    \item What we can see in the data is the \textbf{Observed difference} between groups
\begin{equation*}
    \underbrace{\EE [Y_i \vert D_i = 1] - \EE [Y_i \vert D_i = 0]}_{\text{Observed difference in outcomes}}
\end{equation*}
    \item What we want to know is the unobservable difference in potential outcomes:
\end{itemize}
\begin{align*}
\text{Average treatment effect (ATE)}  =& \EE [Y_i (1) - Y_i (0)] \\
\text{Average treatment effect on the treated (ATT) } =& \EE [Y_i (1) - Y_i (0) \vert D_i = 1]
\end{align*}
To see why we need independence of treatment assignment and potential outcomes, it helps to use the \textbf{switching equation}:
\begin{equation*}
Y_i = D_i \cdot Y_i (1) + (1 - D_i) Y_i (0)
\end{equation*}
\begin{itemize}
\item [$\implies$] Switching equation lets you go between potential outcomes $\left( \{Y_i (0), Y_i (1)\}\right)$ and observed outcome $Y_i$
\end{itemize}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Na{\"i}ve comparison: $\EE [Y \vert D = 1] - \EE [Y \vert D=0]$}


 Recall that:
% \begin{itemize}
%     \item The expectations operator $\EE [\cdot]$ is the average of a random variable
%     \item The conditional expectations operator $\EE [\cdot \vert \cdot]$ takes the average over particular values. So $\EE [Y \vert D=1]$ is the average value of $Y$ when for observations where $D = 1$
%     \item The math symbol for ``because'' is $\because$
% \end{itemize}
\begin{equation*}
Y_i = D_i \cdot Y_i (1) + (1 - D_i) Y_i (0)
\end{equation*}

Let's consider the averages of $Y_{1i}$, $Y_{0i}$, and $Y_i$:
\begin{align*}
\EE [ Y_i \vert D_i = 1 ] =& \EE [D_i Y_i (1) + (1 - D_i) Y_i (0) \vert D = 1]
&&\because Y_i = D_i Y_i (1) + (1 - D_i) Y_i (0) \\
=& \alert<3>{\EE[Y_i (1) \vert D_i = 1]}\\
\EE[Y \vert D = 0] 
% =& E [Y_0 + (Y_1 - Y_0) D \vert D = 0] \\
=& \alert<3>{\EE[Y_i (0) \vert D_i = 0]}
\end{align*}

\vspace{2ex}

\begin{frame}{Causal effects in randomized experiment}

Observed difference in outcomes equals causal effect of interest plus selection bias:
\begin{align*}
\underbrace{\EE [Y_i \vert D_i = 1] - \EE [Y_i \vert D_i = 0]}_{\text{Observed difference in outcomes}}
% =& \EE [Y_1 \vert D=1] { - \EE [Y_0 \vert D = 1] + \EE [Y_0 \vert D = 1]}  - \EE [Y_0 \vert D=0] \\
=& \underbrace{\EE [Y_i (1) - Y_i (0) \vert D_i = 1]}_{\text{Avg.  treatment effect on treated}} 
+ \underbrace{\EE [Y_i (0) \vert D_i = 1] - \EE [Y_i (0) \vert D_i=0]}_{\text{Selection bias}}
\end{align*}

Consider the section bias when measuring the effect of college attendance on wages:
\begin{itemize}
\item $\EE [Y_i (0) \vert D_i = 1]$: the wage someone went to college would have \emph{if they hadn't gone}
\item $\EE [Y_i (0) \vert D_i=0]$: the wage of someone who didn't go to college
\end{itemize}
These objects are equal under independence of treatment assignment ($D_i$) and potential outcomes $\left( \{Y_i (0), Y_i (1)\}\right)$

\vspace{2ex}
Further, under randomization ATT = ATE! (this is less important than the selection point above, but a nice-to-have)
\begin{align*}
    \EE [Y_i \vert D_i = 1] - \EE [Y_i \vert D_i=0]  
    =& \EE [Y_i (1) \vert D_i = 1] - \EE [Y_i (0) \vert D_i = 0] \\
    =& \EE [Y_i (1) \vert D_i = 1] - \EE [Y_i (0) \vert D_i = 1] \\
    =& \EE [Y_i (1) - Y_i (0) \vert D_i = 1] \\
    =& \EE [Y_i (1) - Y_i (0)] \\
    =& \EE [Y_i (1)] - \EE [Y_i (0)]
    % =& \EE [Y_1 - Y_0 \vert D = 1] + \EE [Y_0 \vert D = 1] - \EE [Y_0 \vert D=0] \\
    % =& \EE [Y_1 - Y_0 \vert D = 1] \\
    % =& \EE [Y_1 \vert D = 1]
\end{align*}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Selection bias}

\begin{align*}
\underbrace{\EE [Y_i \vert D_i = 1] - \EE [Y_i \vert D_i = 0]}_{\text{Observed difference in outcomes}}
% =& \EE [Y_1 \vert D=1] { - \EE [Y_0 \vert D = 1] + \EE [Y_0 \vert D = 1]}  - \EE [Y_0 \vert D=0] \\
=& \underbrace{\EE [Y_i (1) - Y_i (0) \vert D_i = 1]}_{\text{Avg.  treatment effect on treated}} 
+ \underbrace{\EE [Y_i (0) \vert D_i = 1] - \EE [Y_i (0) \vert D_i=0]}_{\text{Selection bias}}
\end{align*}
BUT there's often reason to believe that selection bias in non-zero
\begin{itemize}
\item What if people who go to college are ``higher ability'' (broadly defined)
\item [$\implies$] They would earn a higher wage than those in the control group, even if they hadn't gone to college:
\begin{equation*}
\EE [Y_i (0) \vert D_i = 1] > \EE [Y_i (0) \vert D_i = 0]
\end{equation*}
\item [$\implies$] Selection bias is positive
\end{itemize}
Let's consider a simplified case where selection bias is non-zero
\end{frame}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Suppose people are ``high ability'' or ``low ability''
    \item Assume treatment (college attendance) is as good as randomly assigned, conditional on ability, $X_i$
\end{itemize}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Finding the average effect for high ability people
    \item Finding the average effect for low ability people
    \item Weighing these average effects, based on the distribution of ability in the population
\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}{Selection bias under conditional independence assumption (CIA)}

Observed difference in outcomes equals causal effect of interest plus selection bias:
\begin{multline*}
\underbrace{\EE [Y_i \vert D_i = 1, \alert{X_i}] - \EE [Y_i \vert D_i = 0,  \alert{X_i}]}_{\text{Observed difference in outcomes given $X_i$}} \\
% =& \EE [Y_1 \vert D=1] { - \EE [Y_0 \vert D = 1] + \EE [Y_0 \vert D = 1]}  - \EE [Y_0 \vert D=0] \\
= \underbrace{\EE [Y_i (1) - Y_i (0) \vert D_i = 1, \alert{X_i}]}_{\text{Avg.  treatment effect on treated}} 
+ \underbrace{\EE [Y_i (0) \vert D_i = 1, \alert{X_i}] - \EE [Y_i (0) \vert D_i=0, \alert{X_i}]}_{\text{Selection bias}}
\end{multline*}

Under CIA, selection bias disappears conditional on $X_i$:
\begin{align*}
    \underbrace{\underbrace{\EE [Y_{i} (0) \vert X_i, D_i = 1]}_{\text{Control potential outcome for treated group}} =\underbrace{ \EE [Y_{i} (0) \vert X_i, D_i = 0]}_{\text{Outcome for control group}}}_{\text{Selection bias equals zero}}
\end{align*}
$\implies$ Difference in observed outcomes \textbf{conditional on $X_i$} equals causal effect of interest:
\begin{align*}
    \EE [Y_i \vert X_i, D_i = 1] - \EE [Y_i \vert X_i, D_i = 0] 
    % &= \EE [Y_{1i} \vert X_i, D_i = 1] - \EE [Y_{0i} \vert X_i, D_i = 0] \\
    % &= \EE [Y_{1i} \vert X_i] - \EE [Y_{0i} \vert X_i] \\
    &= \EE [Y_{i} (1) - Y_{i} (0) \vert D_i = 1, X_i]
\end{align*}
Given the CIA, comparisons of observed outcomes $Y_i$ (conditional on $X_i$) have a causal interpretation
\begin{itemize}
    \item Holding $X_i$ contact, treatment assignment \textbf{looks} random
    \item Selection bias disappears
\end{itemize}

\end{frame}
